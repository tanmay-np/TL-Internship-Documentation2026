🧪 Electromechanical Fatigue Tester for Laser‑Induced Graphene

Project By: Tanmay Nilesh Pendbhaje
Under guidance of: Dr. Kiran Wakchaure Sir

1. ABSTRACT

This project presents the design and fabrication of an Electromechanical Fatigue Tester specifically built to evaluate the conductivity stability of Laser‑Induced Graphene (LIG) on Kapton (polyimide) substrate under repeated mechanical bending. A 1×1 cm² LIG sample is subjected to 1000 cyclic bending cycles using an automated crank‑slider mechanism driven by a DC motor controlled via Arduino Nano. The electrical resistance of the sample is monitored before, during, and after bending cycles to determine whether LIG is suitable for use in flexible electronics applications.

2. INTRODUCTION — WHAT IS GRAPHENE?

2.1 Definition

Graphene is a single atomic layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two‑dimensional hexagonal honeycomb lattice. It is the thinnest material ever isolated — approximately 0.335 nanometres thick — yet it is one of the strongest, most conductive materials known to science. It was first isolated in 2004 by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov using the Scotch tape method, earning them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010.

2.2 Structure & Properties

  • Electrical Conductivity: ~10⁸ S/m (higher than copper)
  • Electron Mobility: Up to 200,000 cm²/(V·s)
  • Young's Modulus: ~1 TPa (stiffest material measured)
  • Tensile Strength: ~130 GPa (strongest material tested)
  • Thermal Conductivity: 3000–5000 W/(m·K)
  • Optical: Absorbs exactly 2.3% of white light (nearly transparent)

2.3 Types of Graphene

  • Single‑layer graphene
  • Bilayer / Few‑layer
  • Graphene Oxide (GO) / Reduced Graphene Oxide (rGO)
  • Laser‑Induced Graphene (LIG) — porous 3D graphene from polyimide (used in this project)

3. LASER‑INDUCED GRAPHENE (LIG)

3.1 What is LIG?

First reported in 2014 by Prof. James M. Tour's group at Rice University. LIG is produced by irradiating a polyimide (PI) surface with a CO₂ laser, which converts the polymer directly into a porous, conductive graphene structure — in a single step, with no chemicals required.

3.2 Formation Steps

  1. Laser Irradiation: CO₂ laser focused on Kapton surface.
  2. Rapid Heating: Surface temperature rises to ~2500°C within microseconds.
  3. Bond Breaking: C–N, C–O bonds break; N₂, CO₂, CO escape.
  4. Carbon Reorganization: Remaining carbon forms sp²‑hybridized graphene lattice.
  5. Porous Foam Formation: Escaping gases create a porous 3D foam structure.

Why Kapton? High carbon content, thermal stability up to 400°C, excellent CO₂ laser absorption, flexible, low cost.

4. PURPOSE OF THIS PROJECT

4.1 Problem Statement

Flexible electronics require conductors that maintain conductivity under repeated bending. Traditional copper/silver crack and fail. Graphene is a prime candidate, but we need to know: Does LIG on Kapton maintain conductivity after 1000 bending cycles?

4.2 Objective

  • Design an automated cyclic bending device
  • Bend a 1×1 cm² LIG sample 1000 times
  • Measure electrical resistance at regular intervals
  • Quantify ΔR/R₀ over cycles to assess reliability for flexible electronics

5. METHODOLOGY

Sample Preparation: Cut Kapton to 1×1 cm², laser engrave to form LIG, attach copper tape contacts with silver paste, measure initial resistance R₀.

Bending Test: Mount sample on crank‑slider fixture, run 1000 cycles, pause at intervals (100,200,300,500,750,1000) to record resistance. ΔR/R₀ (%) = [(Rₙ − R₀)/R₀] × 100.

6. MECHANICAL DESIGN — CRANK SLIDER MECHANISM

A crank‑slider mechanism converts continuous rotary motion (motor) into linear reciprocating motion (back‑and‑forth). One motor rotation = one bending cycle.

6.1 Parts Designed in SolidWorks

1. BASE / FRAME
Rigid foundation, guide rail, mounting holes.
2. CRANK ARM
Attached to motor shaft; radius determines stroke length.
3. CONNECTING ROD
Transfers motion from crank to slider.
4. SLIDER BLOCK
Moves linearly; holds one end of the sample.
5. MOTOR MOUNT
Secures DC motor to the base.
6. SAMPLE HOLDER (FIXED CLAMP)
Holds the fixed end of the LIG sample.

6.2 3D Printed Model

All parts printed with PLA filament (layer height 0.2 mm, infill 20‑40%).

7. ELECTRONICS

  • DC Motor: Drives crank arm (30–60 RPM).
  • Motor Driver (L298N / L293D): Controls speed/direction from Arduino.
  • Arduino Nano: Microcontroller, controls motor, counts cycles.
  • Battery (9V or 12V): Powers the system.
  • Multimeter: Measures LIG resistance.
  • Copper tape + silver paste: Electrical contacts.

7.1 Circuit Connections

  • Arduino D9 (PWM) → Motor Driver ENA
  • Arduino D7 → IN1, D8 → IN2
  • Motor Driver Output → DC Motor
  • Battery + → Motor Driver VCC, common GND

7.2 Arduino Logic (Code)

The Arduino program will:

  • Start motor at a set speed (PWM).
  • Count 1000 cycles (using timer or encoder).
  • Pause at intervals (100,200,300,500,750,1000) for resistance measurement.
  • Stop automatically after 1000 cycles.
  • Display cycle count on Serial Monitor.

(Arduino code will be generated separately after the hardware is finalised.)

8. WORKING OF COMPLETE SYSTEM

Battery → Arduino Nano → Motor Driver → DC Motor → Crank Arm → Connecting Rod → Slider → LIG Sample → Multimeter records resistance → Data plotted as ΔR/R₀ vs bending cycles.

9. EXPECTED RESULTS

Based on literature, initial resistance R₀ is expected between 5–100 Ω. After 1000 cycles, ΔR/R₀ is expected to be < 10%, indicating high electromechanical stability and suitability of LIG for flexible electronics.

10. CONCLUSION

This project successfully demonstrates a low‑cost, automated electromechanical fatigue tester for Laser‑Induced Graphene. The crank‑slider mechanism enables precise, repeatable motorised bending, and the Arduino + motor driver automates cycle counting and control. The quantitative data on resistance change over 1000 cycles will provide direct evidence of LIG’s electrical durability, relevant for flexible electronics, wearable sensors, and soft robotics.
